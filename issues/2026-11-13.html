EscalatingIran war · day 259 (began Feb 28)

Trump says "we don't need a deal," sidelines Vance, and Iran claims a hit on a US radar in Bahrain

What happened

Since Wednesday: on Thursday Trump posted that "We don't need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed," and moved Vance off Iran to focus on crime in Democratic-run cities, a day after the Wall Street Journal reported Vance was weighing face-to-face talks with security chief Mohsen Rezaei in Doha. Trump told reporters "We won. It's just a question of when we end it," and said he wants "total victory, not a 93 percent victory" (Iran International). He also said he'd consider it a defeat to leave office with Iran's current rulers in place (CNN). Iraq refused a US request under Task Force Iron Gate to close all but two border crossings, and Turkey has largely declined to change its border policy; G7 foreign ministers pledged to keep up pressure. At sea, Iran's army said two tankers caught fire after hitting mines in the eastern strait (RFE/RL), and Reuters reported an Iranian attack on navigation equipment on Oman's Didamar island. Overnight, an unattributed air strike hit a telecom tower in Sirik, Hormozgan (IRNA, Iranian state media). Friday morning the IRGC said it "targeted and destroyed" a US early-warning radar in Bahrain, its first claim against US assets in a Gulf state since early September (Al Jazeera). Neither the US nor Bahrain had commented when this was built. A confidential IAEA report says Iran told the agency inspection talks can't resume under military threat (The New Arab).

Why it matters

The diplomatic track that moved oil prices down last week has lost its US lead and its political cover. If the Bahrain strike is confirmed, it's the first direct Iranian hit on a US site in a Gulf ally in two months, which would test Trump's "not hesitate" warning.

How each side sees it

Tehran: no talks, no transfer

Rezaei: "We are not negotiating with the Americans at present," and enriched material "will remain in Iran," ruling out shipping it to Russia. IRGC chief Ahmad Vahidi promises "devastating and painful strikes" for any US attack.

Via Iran International (diaspora, anti-regime) · The New Arab (Qatari-owned)
Washington: victory, not a deal

Trump: "We've got their Strait!" and he would "not hesitate" to strike again. The administration's bet is that the blockade and Iron Gate squeeze harder than any agreement would deliver.

Via CNBC (mainstream) · CNN (mainstream)
Skeptics: neither war nor peace

Analyst Shayan Samii calls the approach "neither war nor negotiation." Responsible Statecraft's Blaise Malley notes Democrats haven't agreed how to use the Dec 11 funding bill, their first post-midterm lever on the war.

Via RFE/RL (US-funded) · Responsible Statecraft (restraint)
Watch: US or Bahraini confirmation of the radar strike and any US response; whether Qatar's interim plan (Iran halts shipping attacks, US eases the port blockade) survives without Vance; more mines in the eastern strait.

EscalatingSaudi–Houthi war

Houthis aim at Saudi Arabia's Hormuz bypass; the UN renews Yemen sanctions

What happened

Since Wednesday: late Wednesday the Saudi-led coalition said it destroyed two ballistic missiles aimed at western Saudi Arabia, plus drones in the south and Mecca region; civil defense said debris fell in Sabouha, about 35 km from Mecca. The Houthis called the claim they targeted Mecca "a grave falsehood" (Al Jazeera). On Thursday Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree claimed a drone strike on Pumping Station No. 7 of the East-West pipeline and a missile on the Yanbu oil terminal, plus Sharurah airport, Khamis Mushait air base and Aramco's Riyadh headquarters. The coalition reported intercepting drones over Riyadh and the Eastern Province and didn't confirm any hits (Middle East Eye). The pipeline carries about 4 million barrels a day to Yanbu, the main route around Hormuz. Also Thursday, the UN Security Council unanimously renewed Yemen sanctions and its expert panel for a year, condemning Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia. OCHA counts more than 333,000 displaced inside Yemen since early August, 24,000 in the past week. Another drone was intercepted over the Eastern Province Friday (House of Saud).

Why it matters

With Hormuz still constrained, the East-West pipeline and Yanbu are how much Saudi crude reaches the market. Damage there would hit oil prices harder than anything in the strait now.

How each side sees it

Riyadh: defenses are holding

Coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki reports interceptions only; Saudi officials said Monday's pipeline fire didn't affect operations. Pro-Saudi outlets stress the coalition's offensive toward Sanaa.

Via House of Saud (pro-Saudi) · Al Jazeera (Qatar-funded)

Via Middle East Eye (Arab-world, critical of Riyadh) · The New Arab (Qatari-owned)

DevelopingEnergy / diesel crisis

Oil jumps on Trump's "no deal" post as the IEA says the market is tipping into surplus

What happened

Since Wednesday: Brent rose $3.06 to $95.82 Thursday and WTI $2.21 to $88.74 after Trump's post (CNBC). Friday morning the IEA said the market returns to surplus this quarter, with supply about 1.7 million barrels a day above demand, reversing last month's deficit forecast; it cut 2026 demand by 2.7 million b/d to 102.3 million and sees a 2027 surplus near 3.8 million b/d. It said Gulf exports are recovering through "dark crossings" of Hormuz, and that 71 million barrels from March's emergency release, 15 million of it diesel, will be available by end-February (Reuters via Investing.com). OPEC's monthly report left demand forecasts unchanged and showed OPEC+ crude output up 2.04 million b/d in October to 41.23 million, with Saudi Arabia up about 1.2 million (Newsquawk; allAfrica); some analysts suspect the Saudi jump is partly a reporting quirk. Ukraine hit Russia's largest refinery, Omsk, Wednesday. Kpler data cited secondhand put Hormuz transits at 13 Tuesday and 7 Wednesday; no LNG carrier has crossed since July.

Why it matters

Physical supply is improving while political risk is rising again. Diesel, still above $6, depends on which wins.

How each side sees it

IEA: "The oil market is showing tentative signs of rebalancing." With surplus forecast through 2027, OPEC+ "faces an extremely difficult call" on quotas Nov 29, says Rystad's Jorge Leon.

DevelopingCOP31

At least 14 countries sign the electrification pledge; the Arab Group fights the fossil roadmap

What happened

Since Wednesday: the leaders' summit in Antalya ended with at least 14 countries backing the "35 by 35" pledge to raise electricity's share of final energy from about 23% to 35% by 2035, including Australia, Germany, Norway, Finland, South Korea and Pakistan. The EU is expected to join; the UK welcomed it without signing, Brazil wants changes and Saudi Arabia objects to electrification as the main route. The pledge is non-binding and doesn't say how the power is generated (Climate Home News, via WINSS). About 40 leaders spoke; the heads of the US, France, Germany, Italy, Japan and Canada stayed away, and the US has no official delegation for a second year. Guterres said it's "long past time" to stop treating the transition from fossil fuels as taboo (UN News). The Saudi-led Arab Group opposes any mention of Brazil's voluntary fossil-transition roadmap in the summit's decisions (House of Saud). Finland published a national phase-out plan.

Why it matters

Electrification is the one framing big emitters and oil producers might both accept, which is why fights over the word "clean" and the roadmap will decide what Antalya actually delivers.

From "effectively over" to "total victory": what Trump's Iran war has become

On Monday, Trump said the war with Iran was "militarily, effectively over." On Thursday he said he didn't need a deal and wanted "total victory, not a 93 percent victory." Both statements fit the same policy. Since early November the US has stopped bombing at scale and started besieging, and this week showed the siege has no agreed endpoint.

How we got here. The war began Feb 28. For most of the year, Hormuz was largely shut, Gulf exports collapsed and diesel passed $6. By autumn, US strikes had become occasional, like the Nov 2 raid on Qeshm Island, and the main pressure was economic: a naval blockade of Iran's ports and now Operation Iron Gate, aimed at its land borders and Caspian trade. Iran's terms haven't moved. It wants seven conditions met before talks, including an end to the blockade, the release of frozen assets and a halt to fighting on all fronts. Qatar, with Pakistan, has been pushing a smaller interim deal: Iran stops attacking shipping, and the US eases the port blockade.

What changed this week. Three things. First, the US envoy left the room. Vance had reportedly been weighing a meeting with Iran's security chief Mohsen Rezaei in Doha; Trump reassigned him to domestic crime. Second, the siege showed its limits. Iraq refused a US request to close all but two border crossings, and Turkey has largely declined to change its rules, so Iron Gate depends on neighbors who trade with Iran. Third, the shooting at sea picked up. Mines set tankers on fire in the eastern strait, Iran hit navigation gear on an Omani island, and on Friday the Revolutionary Guards claimed they destroyed a US radar in Bahrain. That claim is unverified, but it's their first claimed strike on a US site in a Gulf state since September. Iran's Assembly of Experts has called for a review of its membership in the Non-Proliferation Treaty, and Iran has told the IAEA that inspection talks can't resume under threat.

The case for the siege. The administration argues pressure is working without US casualties. Rubio said last week the economic pain on Iran "compounds," and that there's "no need for major combat operations." The IEA's report this morning gives that view some support: Gulf exports are recovering through "dark crossings" of the strait, and the agency now sees a global surplus. If oil can flow around Iran, Washington loses little by waiting.

The case against it. Restraint advocates say a siege with no diplomatic exit invites the slow escalation seen this week. Each mine, each tanker fire and each claimed strike on a Gulf base is a chance for a wider war, and the Bahrain claim would test Trump's promise that he would "not hesitate" to strike again. Analyst Shayan Samii calls it "neither war nor negotiation." From the center-right, The Dispatch's Jonathan Ruhe argues the war has "brought our adversaries closer together," meaning Russia, China and North Korea. Quincy's Trita Parsi warned after the election that the lame-duck months could bring more pressure for strikes, not less. In Tehran, hardliners read "no deal" as proof that talking was pointless. Rezaei says enriched uranium "will remain in Iran," which rules out the Russia transfer idea.

The politics at home. The midterms, which Vance himself blamed on the war, gave Democrats both chambers starting in January. Until then, their leverage is the Dec 11 funding bill. Kaine is drafting language to end the war; Tom Cole says a Republican Congress won't send Trump bills he would veto. That makes a Christmas shutdown over the war possible, and it gives Trump until January before Congress can force the question.

The oil market's bet. Traders have mostly priced a settlement. Brent fell about 20% in November before Thursday's 3% jump. The IEA's surplus forecast gives that bet a fundamental floor. The risk sits around Hormuz, at Saudi Arabia's Yanbu bypass under Houthi fire, and in a single confirmed hit on a US base.

What to watch. Whether the US confirms the Bahrain strike and how it responds. Whether Qatar's interim plan survives without Vance. Whether Trump and Xi discuss Iran at APEC next week, since China is Iran's biggest oil customer. And the Dec 11 vote, the first time the new antiwar majority will have a real lever.
