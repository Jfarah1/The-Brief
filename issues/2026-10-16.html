EscalatingIran war · day 231 (began Feb 28)

Two ships sunk near Hormuz as the shipping war hits its peak

What happened

Since Wednesday: the Singapore-flagged container ship GFS Pride, carrying about 2,500 containers, sank near the strait on Wednesday; Singapore's port authority says all 21 crew were rescued. The IRGC says the ship hit a mine on an "unauthorised" route; CENTCOM says an IRGC projectile hit it and it went down under tow. The cargo ship Cape Dao, struck on Sept 23 in an attack that killed one crew member, also sank. Critical Threats/ISW calls them the first Iranian sinkings since March. Gulf News counts at least 10 ships struck since Sunday, the most of the war. Iranian media reported tanker fires on Wednesday, Thursday and again this morning, each a ship that had gone dark in what the IRGC calls its "prohibited zone"; none is independently confirmed (Euronews, Al Jazeera). CENTCOM says more than 40 vessels transited in a day, but IMF PortWatch counted 2 on Oct 11, against about 85 a day before the war. In Washington, Treasury sanctioned 16 people and 10 entities Thursday, and CNN reports the Pentagon has drawn up options to use its largest conventional bomb against Iranian energy and nuclear sites. Trump told Newsmax "I wouldn't use nuclear" and said ground troops aren't planned. ISW logged "nothing significant" on talks both days; we found no US counter-offer. Netanyahu said a pre-emptive Israeli strike before his Oct 27 election is "no good."

Why it matters

Sinking ships is a step up from damaging them, and it comes with insurers already pricing freight at about a third of cargo value (OilPrice). An Iranian official told Reuters Tehran aims to keep oil high before the midterms. Trump's no-strikes-before-Nov-3 pledge from last week now sits alongside Pentagon strike planning.

How each side sees it

Tehran: we control the strait

IRGC Navy chief Azmayi claims "smart control" of Hormuz and says every ship needs permission. President Pezeshkian: "Bombs and missiles can't force a nation to surrender." Speaker Ghalibaf says Iran must prepare for war while negotiating because Washington "breaks its promises."

Via Euronews (European) · Times of Israel (Israeli, center) · Fars via Fox News
Hawks: Iran is changing tactics

ISW says Iran is "deploying new tactics," shifting the type and pace of attacks; CENTCOM insists US forces "clearly control" the strait. Treasury's Bessent: "The noose is tightening around Iran's economy."

Via Critical Threats / ISW (hawkish think tanks) · Al Jazeera (Qatar-funded)
The Gulf: lasting damage

The IMF's Jihad Azour told Gulf News that Gulf economies won't return to their pre-war path; the IMF now sees the Middle East and Central Asia region shrinking 0.3% this year. Qatar is calling for the strait to reopen unconditionally and says it is still mediating.

Via Gulf News (UAE)
Watch: Any order to strike energy sites despite the pledge; more sinkings; a written US reply to Iran's seven conditions; ISW's report of Iran telling Hezbollah to prepare for conflict (Emirati media, unconfirmed).

EscalatingSaudi–Houthi war · truce collapsed in July

Houthis strike Yemen's presidential palace in Aden

What happened

Since Wednesday: Houthi missiles hit the al-Maashiq presidential palace in Aden on Thursday, where Yemen's internationally recognized government sits. The information minister says five missiles were fired. Tolls differ: Al Jazeera, citing the health minister, reports 5 killed and 16 wounded; The New Arab and Reuters say at least 4 killed. The prime minister was unhurt; the Houthis say they targeted a coalition operations center in the compound. Thursday evening they fired 9 ballistic missiles and 7 other projectiles at Marib, and fighting continues around Taiz. Wednesday, Saudi Arabia intercepted a missile aimed at Riyadh, and the Houthis told civilians and workers to leave Saudi airports and oil sites. A Houthi missile hit a Saudi Eurofighter over Saada; the jet apparently survived (ISW). The coalition says Riyadh's airport has been attacked 20 times since Oct 1, with 17 killed. France began deploying assets to protect Saudi oil facilities, and the EU-Gulf summit set for Riyadh on Oct 24 was postponed. There is still no US decision on joining.

Why it matters

Hitting Aden widens the war from Saudi soil back into Yemen's own civil war, and threatens the government Riyadh is fighting to restore. With Hormuz flows already crippled, every strike on Saudi energy sites matters more to oil.

How each side sees it

Riyadh and Aden: foreign-supplied aggression

Coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki says the Houthis are getting ballistic missiles from other countries in the region, and Riyadh says there is no truce while they hold Bab al-Mandeb.

Via Al Jazeera (Qatar-funded) · France 24 (French public)
Sanaa: hitting the war's command

The Houthis say the palace held a coalition operations headquarters. Earlier this week their official al-Bukhaiti said they "would welcome" mediated talks, on their own terms.

Via The New Arab (Qatari-owned)
Analysts: Saudi deterrence is eroding

Al Jazeera's correspondent calls the attack the first of its kind and warns of a return to all-out civil war; ISW reads the Aden and Marib barrages as an effort to weaken the government.

Via Al Jazeera · Critical Threats / ISW (hawkish)
Watch: Riyadh airport reopening after Saturday; any US decision; the Taiz and Bab al-Mandeb fronts.

DevelopingFed tightening cycle

A huge PPI print, yet October hike bets fade

What happened

Since Wednesday: producer prices rose 1.3% in September, the biggest jump since March 2022, and 6.3% from a year earlier, against forecasts near 0.5% and 5.3%. Core PPI rose 0.7% (5.0% a year); energy rose 10.8% and drove more than 80% of the rise in goods (BLS). Retail sales rose 0.8% against 0.3% expected, but the control group, which feeds GDP, rose only 0.1%. Jobless claims were 199,000, a fifth straight week under 200,000. Wednesday's hot CPI briefly pushed the 10-year above 5.3% and the S&P down 1.1%. Then markets turned: the 10-year closed Thursday at 5.10%. Reported odds of an October hike fell from roughly half after CPI to about 15% by Friday morning (investingLive); readings differ by source. Fed Chair Warsh, at the IMF overnight, gave no signal on October and said he won't pre-judge meetings (FXStreet, AP). Chicago's Goolsbee called September "a disappointing month for inflation."

How each side sees it

Markets: it's an oil shock, and growth is the risk

Traders treated the PPI as energy pass-through and focused on weak core retail spending. investingLive called it "a minor dovish repricing," with one more hike priced by year-end; Goldman reads Warsh as consistent with an October pause and a December hike.

Via investingLive (markets news) · FXStreet (markets news)
Inflation hawks: it's spreading

Goolsbee called September "a disappointing month for inflation" and warned that if AI-driven demand overheats the economy, it "must be addressed." Core producer prices rose 5.0% from a year earlier.

Via FXStreet · BLS PPI release (primary)
Watch: Fed blackout from Saturday; FOMC Oct 27–28; whether the 10-year tests 5.00%.

Long read

~5 min

A two-front war on Gulf oil

For most of the 231 days of the Iran war, the danger to oil supply came from one direction: the Strait of Hormuz, through which about 17 million barrels a day flowed before the fighting began (Kpler, via Al Jazeera). This week that danger hit a new level, and a second front, the Saudi–Houthi war to the southwest, became a real threat to energy supply. Between them, the two conflicts now squeeze the Gulf's oil exports from both ends.

How we got here. The US naval blockade of Iranian oil and Iran's harassment of shipping have run side by side since spring. Through the summer, Gulf exports other than Iran's mostly recovered: Kpler put them above 81% of pre-war levels in September. Then, in early October, the IRGC began enforcing what it calls a "prohibited zone," attacking ships that run with their transponders off or take routes it hasn't approved. UKMTO recorded 15 attacks between Oct 6 and 14. On Wednesday the campaign crossed a line, as two cargo ships sank, the first sinkings since March by Critical Threats' count. IMF PortWatch logged two transits on Oct 11, against about 85 a day before the war, although CENTCOM says more than 40 ships passed in a recent 24-hour period. Dark transits make every count unreliable, and that uncertainty is part of what makes insurers nervous. Freight now runs to about a third of cargo value (OilPrice).

The Yemen front has a separate origin. A Saudi-brokered truce with the Houthis collapsed in July, and a Saudi-backed push toward Bab al-Mandeb followed. The Houthis answered by striking Saudi airports, then on Oct 13 an Aramco building in Dhahran, and on Thursday the presidential palace in Aden. Riyadh says there will be no truce while the Houthis hold the strait at the Red Sea's mouth. Saudi Arabia's main workaround for Hormuz, its East-West pipeline to Red Sea ports, ends on the coast the Houthis can reach.

Four ways to read it. The hawkish reading, from ISW and the US command, is that Iran is changing tactics because it is losing the larger contest. The blockade is the tightest Iran has faced since 1979, President Pezeshkian himself concedes, and Treasury keeps adding sanctions. On this view, the sinkings are escalation from weakness, and the answer is more pressure. The Pentagon's reported strike options against energy sites follow that logic.

Tehran's reading is the mirror image. IRGC commanders claim "smart control" of the strait, and an Iranian official told Reuters the goal is to keep prices high ahead of the US midterms. Speaker Ghalibaf says Iran must prepare for war while negotiating, because American promises can't be trusted. Iran, in this telling, is using the one lever that reaches American voters directly: the price of fuel.

The restraint camp, including writers at Responsible Statecraft, has argued for months that Washington's strategy of keeping the conflict at a manageable boil until the election only sets up a larger clash afterward. They see Riyadh's anti-Houthi campaign as a similar dead end that may end with Saudi Arabia negotiating on Houthi terms. By the coalition's own count, Riyadh's airport has been attacked 20 times this month.

The Gulf's own reading is economic. The IMF's Jihad Azour told Gulf News the region's economies will not return to their pre-war path, and the IMF now expects the wider Middle East and Central Asia to shrink this year. Qatar is calling for Hormuz to reopen unconditionally. France is deploying assets to protect Saudi oil sites, and the EU-Gulf summit planned for Riyadh was postponed. For the Gulf states, the cost of the war is being paid now, not after Nov 3.

What cushions remain. Very few. The IEA has released about 330 million of its 400-million-barrel pledge and on Thursday added no new volumes. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve sits near 283 million barrels, its lowest since 1982. US crude stocks fell 8.6 million barrels last week because of Gulf of Mexico shut-ins. OPEC's Gulf members are already pumping below their quotas, so spare capacity that can't reach the sea isn't really spare. The Russian diesel waiver, meant to fill one gap, has yet to deliver a confirmed cargo.

What to watch. First, whether Trump's pre-election pledge survives his own Pentagon's options. Second, whether the Houthis turn from Aden and Riyadh to Red Sea export terminals such as Yanbu. That is the scenario that would close both ends of Saudi Arabia's escape route at once. Third, the price itself. Brent has held near $106 through all of this. For a market that has lost most of Hormuz's flow, that suggests traders still expect it back. If that expectation breaks, the price will move faster than any release can catch it.
