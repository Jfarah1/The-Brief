NewUS–China–Russia summitry

Shenzhen's three-way summit produces a "Declaration on Peace" and no arms limits

What happened

Since Wednesday: Trump, Xi and Putin met for more than an hour Thursday in Shenzhen, the first-ever leaders' meeting of the three powers. They issued a four-point "China-Russia-U.S. Declaration on Peace." It calls for UN reform; for more production and supply of refined petroleum products; for respecting international law, including immunity for state officials, with a rebuke of the International Criminal Court's "abuse of power" (the ICC has a warrant out for Putin); and for dialogue on nuclear risk and nonproliferation. It sets no arms reductions, framework or timetable. The White House said the leaders reaffirmed that "a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought" (RFA via Eurasia Review). New START lapsed in February. China has more than 600 warheads, against more than 5,000 each for the US and Russia, and it says the other two must cut first. Xi proposed regular three-way exchanges and a crisis-communication channel (Xinhua and CCTV, Chinese state media). Before the three-way, Trump and Putin met for about 40 minutes. Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said Ukraine was the main topic, and Trump said "things are happening" (Kyiv Independent). At their bilateral, Xinhua says Trump told Xi the US "does not support Taiwan independence"; the White House hasn't confirmed that. Trump described a "30-for-30" deal cutting tariffs on $30 billion of trade (Fox News). No chip deal was announced, and there's no sign a Kim Jong Un meeting took place. China's new export licensing on drone parts used by Russia took effect Wednesday.

Why it matters

The format itself is the result: the three leaders now have a channel, but nothing binds them to use it. Moscow got an anti-ICC line, Beijing a possible Taiwan statement, and Washington a small tariff deal. Ukraine and Europe weren't represented.

How each side sees it

Beijing and Moscow: a new model of great-power talks

A Global Times editorial calls it a "starting point": the three "have different national conditions, but this doesn't mean confrontation is inevitable." It names Taiwan China's "first red line." Putin: "we hold joint responsibility for maintaining international peace and security."

Via Global Times (Chinese state media) · NBC News (mainstream)
Washington: history made

Trump: "We are really making history." He conceded that "once you have the nuclear weapons, it's very hard to not have them."

Via Fox News (mainstream, right)
Kyiv and Europe's flank: a club without us

Ukraine's Andrii Sybiha says a format including Ukraine "could help end the war sooner," and that this one encourages Putin to keep fighting. Poland's Donald Tusk: "God, protect us from such friends." Crisis Group's Ali Wyne says Trump and Putin "are currying favor with President Xi."

Via Kyiv Independent (Ukrainian) · NBC News

EscalatingUkraine war / peace track

Europe asks for a winter energy truce; Putin answers with one of the war's largest barrages

What happened

Since Wednesday: the Coalition of the Willing met in Berlin Thursday, hosted by Merz, Macron and Britain's Andy Burnham, with Zelensky and US envoys Witkoff and Kushner. Its statement asks Russia for "an energy ceasefire for the duration of the winter" and a Black Sea food-export truce. Future talks are expected to include Europeans for the first time (Kyiv Independent). Kushner said of Article 5-style guarantees, "we've gotten the Russians comfortable with that concept." Russia's deputy foreign minister Sergei Ryabkov says any "quasi membership" of NATO is unacceptable (Al Jazeera). At his Shenzhen press conference Putin called the Crimean Bridge damage "serious" and said of the truces, "we will not negotiate... We will respond" (Kyiv Independent). Overnight into Friday, Russia launched 84 cruise missiles, 27 ballistic missiles and 404 drones, per Ukraine's Air Force. It says 79 cruise missiles, 3 ballistic missiles and 380 drones were downed. At least 12 people were killed and 82 injured. Kyiv's Podilskyi, Southern and Darnytskyi bridges and the 750-kV Zakhidnoukrainska substation were targeted. Poland says two Kh-101 missiles and a drone fell on its territory. Ukraine hit a Druzhba pipeline station in Tatarstan. Zelensky says a swap of more than 1,000 prisoners per side is expected within one to two weeks.

Why it matters

Missiles falling in Poland bring NATO territory back into the war's direct path. Rejecting the energy truce on the same day Berlin proposed it means a winter of strikes on the grid on both sides.

How each side sees it

Kyiv: cut the money off

Zelensky: "If you want to stop war, stop trade with Russia." He says a real Article 5 guarantee needs US and European troops and bases on the ground, and that Ukraine needs €115 billion next year, of which about €90 billion is secured.

Via Kyiv Independent (Ukrainian)
Moscow: no NATO by another name

Ryabkov rejects "quasi membership" of NATO, and Putin complains that "no one wants to discuss Russia's security."

Via Al Jazeera (Qatar-funded)
Analysts: guarantees on paper

The German Marshall Fund's Ian Lesser: there's "nothing automatic about Article 5." Rasmussen Global's Harry Nedelcu says Washington is pressing Kyiv rather than Moscow.

Via Al Jazeera
Watch: Poland's investigation and any NATO consultation; the swap; whether Europeans get a seat at the next round (Merz floated the UAE).

EscalatingFed / inflation

Minutes show a "close call," Fed hawks float a half-point, and stocks fall six days running

What happened

Since Wednesday: the October FOMC minutes show the decision to hold at 3.75–4.00% was a close call for some officials. Nearly all participants expect further hikes to be needed (Federal Reserve; ABA Banking Journal), and a couple said a December move bigger than a quarter point could be warranted if inflation data came in hot (Newsquawk). After the minutes, Governor Christopher Waller said the Fed should either raise rates half a point in December or clearly signal another hike in January (Reuters via BusinessDay). Thursday, Chicago's Austan Goolsbee called inflation "borderline disturbingly high" and wouldn't rule out a larger hike (Bloomberg). The data were mixed. October housing starts jumped 9.9% to a 1.309 million annual rate, but permits fell 7.4% (Census Bureau). Initial jobless claims were 203,000 against 197,000 expected, and continuing claims rose 70,000 to 1.76 million. The Philadelphia Fed's factory index fell to 6.3, its lowest since May, while its prices-paid gauge rose to 58.2 (CNBC). Stocks fell Thursday for a sixth straight session. The S&P 500 lost 2.0% to 7,175.83, the Dow lost 1,101 points to 49,097.06, its first close under 50,000 since June, and the VIX neared 29 (AP via La Nación; CNBC). Bonds rallied as investors sought safety: the 10-year yield was 5.04% Thursday on Treasury's curve and about 4.98% early Friday (Trading Economics).

Why it matters

Markets have priced a December hike for weeks. The new question is size: a half point would be the Fed hiking harder into a falling stock market and a cooling factory sector.

How each side sees it

Hawks: the job isn't done

Goolsbee: "The evidence does not suggest we are on a path back to 2%." He says AI investment is adding to demand. Waller wants either a bigger move or a firm signal of more.

Via Bloomberg (mainstream) · BusinessDay (South African, carrying Reuters)
Skeptics: growth is already cracking

Continuum Economics calls the housing data "mixed but on balance negative," with mortgage rates weighing on permits. Trading Economics reads the five-day bond rally as safe-haven buying, a sign markets fear the economy more than inflation.

Via Census Bureau (primary) · Trading Economics (data)

DevelopingEnergy / diesel crisis

WTI hits a three-month low as Washington says Hormuz flows are at a record

What happened

Since Wednesday: December WTI settled at $83.21 Thursday, its lowest since Aug 26, and it expires today. Brent for January ended flat at $91.98 (Rigzone/Bloomberg; Reuters via Yahoo). Both are on track for a third weekly loss. Energy Secretary Chris Wright said flows through Hormuz are at record levels. Reports from the Shenzhen summit say the US, China and Russia agreed to raise refined-product supply. Wright also said Washington is weighing more diesel and crude exports to Europe. The EIA's weekly report showed builds across the board in the week to Nov 13: crude rose 3.4 million barrels to 417.8 million, gasoline 5.2 million and distillates 1.57 million. Distillates are still about 7% below the five-year average, and refineries ran at 95.7% (EIA). On the risk side, Iran's navy says two tankers hit mines in Hormuz (unverified), Houthi missiles damaged Riyadh's PIF tower, and Reuters sources say Ukraine's drones have cut Russian refinery runs about 45% since late October. Natural gas hit an 11-month high Wednesday on cold forecasts, then gave it back; there's no official settle in our sources. Storage rose 20 Bcf, less than the 27 Bcf expected (EIA). Retail diesel is still $5.937 until Monday's EIA survey.

Why it matters

Oil has fallen about 20% in November on a bet that supply is returning. If that holds, the diesel squeeze should keep easing into winter. But inventories are still thin enough that a single confirmed Gulf hit could reverse it.

How each side sees it

Bears: supply is winning

UBS's Giovanni Staunovo and the IEA see a buyers' market if the conflict resolves quickly. The IEA projects a surplus of about 2 million b/d this quarter, and Goldman warns of oversupply in 2027.

Via Rigzone (energy trade, carrying Bloomberg)

Rabobank's Joe DeLura and Florence Schmit say the market is "not yet at the point where the narrative of improving supply" outweighs the military flare-ups. Saxo's Ole Hansen warns that "liquidity is poor across the markets."

Via Yahoo Finance (mainstream, carrying Reuters) · Rigzone

EscalatingCOP31

COP31's new draft drops fossil fuels entirely, and the summit heads into overtime

What happened

Since Wednesday: the Turkish–Australian co-presidency released a second draft of the cover decision Thursday, which COP31 President Murat Kurum calls the "Antalya Declaration." It is nine pages, down from 18. It drops all language on phasing out or transitioning away from fossil fuels, the welcome for Brazil's fossil-fuel roadmap, the $120 billion-a-year adaptation target and the specific call to speed up electrification. It keeps the 35% electricity share by 2035 in softer wording and acknowledges that warming is projected to pass 1.5°C (RTÉ; Climate Home News). Panama's environment minister Juan Carlos Navarro wrote to UN climate chief Simon Stiell that Panama won't join a consensus without a clear path away from fossil fuels, and he called the talks "an exercise in hypocrisy and futility" (Inside Climate News). Delegations consulted overnight, and Climate Home says the summit, scheduled to end today, is set to run over. EU ministers reconvene today.

Why it matters

The draft would be the first COP outcome to concede 1.5°C will be missed, while dropping the fossil-fuel language agreed in Dubai in 2023. That would be a step back from Dubai.

How each side sees it

Presidency: the only text that can pass

Australia's Chris Bowen called the draft the best basis for agreement. The EU's Wopke Hoekstra declined to judge the missing fossil-fuel language and said he'd test it on adequacy and existing commitments.

Via RTÉ (Irish public broadcaster)
Climate advocates: a regression

A veteran campaigner told Climate Home it is "a glaring regression." Power Shift Africa's Mohamed Adow called it a step backward.

Via Climate Home News (climate diplomacy) · Inside Climate News (climate-advocacy-leaning)

Via Inside Climate News

Three leaders, one table, two wars: what Shenzhen did and didn't settle

For most of the Cold War and after, nuclear diplomacy was a two-way business between Washington and Moscow. On Thursday in Shenzhen it became a three-way meeting for the first time. Trump, Xi and Putin sat together for more than an hour and signed a short "Declaration on Peace." It is the week's most consequential event, less for what it says than for what came around it.

How we got here. New START, the last treaty limiting US and Russian strategic warheads, lapsed in February. China's arsenal has grown past 600 warheads, still far below the more than 5,000 each the US and Russia hold, and Beijing's position hasn't changed: the two big arsenals must shrink before China joins any limits. The trilateral was billed as arms control and Iran. Ukraine was added only after the Crimean Bridge strike. Trump arrived after the first Quad leaders' summit since 2024, in Manila, where the US, Japan, India and Australia launched a maritime-surveillance effort aimed implicitly at China.

What the text says. Four points: UN reform; more production and supply of refined petroleum products; international law, including immunity for state officials, with a rebuke of the International Criminal Court; and dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and nonproliferation. The White House added the old Reagan–Gorbachev formula that a nuclear war "cannot be won and must never be fought." There are no numbers, no verification and no date for a next meeting. Xi proposed regular three-way exchanges and a crisis hotline. Neither is yet agreed in writing.

Who got what. Putin, who faces an ICC arrest warrant, got the US and China to sign a line criticizing the court. That's the declaration's most concrete political gain, and Ukraine wasn't in the room. Xi got to host and, according to Xinhua, heard Trump say the US "does not support Taiwan independence." The White House hasn't confirmed that wording. Trump got a "30-for-30" tariff deal worth about $30 billion of trade, a fuel-supply commitment that helped push WTI to a three-month low, and the image of brokering among the great powers.

The case for it. Supporters argue that a standing channel among the three nuclear powers is worth having for its own sake, especially with New START gone. Tsinghua's Da Wei calls it a "starting point." China's Global Times argues that different systems needn't mean confrontation. Trump frames it as history: "We are really making history."

The case against it. Critics see the format rewarding the two leaders waging or threatening wars. Crisis Group's Ali Wyne says Trump and Putin "are currying favor with President Xi." Ukraine's foreign minister argues that a format without Kyiv encourages Putin to keep fighting. Poland's Tusk was blunter: "God, protect us from such friends." And China's precondition, US and Russian cuts first, is untouched, so the "dialogue" has no agreed agenda.

The test came within hours. The same day, the Coalition of the Willing in Berlin asked Russia for a winter energy ceasefire and a Black Sea truce, and Kushner said Moscow was "comfortable" with Article 5-style guarantees for Ukraine. Putin, speaking in Shenzhen, refused both truces and promised to "respond" to the bridge strike. Overnight Russia fired 111 missiles and 404 drones at Ukraine, killing at least 12, and two cruise missiles fell in Poland. Whatever the summit did for nuclear risk, it didn't slow the war in Europe.

And Iran. The declaration says nothing on Iran. Within a day of Shenzhen, reporting showed Trump weighing a new air campaign, Netanyahu pressing for 4–7 days of strikes, and Trump pointing to "after January 1." China is Iran's biggest oil customer, and Russia is its partner. If Washington strikes, the first test of the new channel will be whether Beijing and Moscow use it, or ignore it.

What to watch. Trump's news conference today for detail on Taiwan and the tariff deal. Any written terms for the hotline Xi proposed. NATO's response to the missiles in Poland. The Jan 10 expiry of the US–China trade truce. And whether a US decision on Iran comes before or after the new Congress takes its seats on Jan 3.
