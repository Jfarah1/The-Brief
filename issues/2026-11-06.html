EscalatingIran war · day 252 (began Feb 28)

Tehran calls the US reply "not convincing" and keeps Hormuz shut; Washington says it's "locked and loaded" but holds fire

What happened

Since Wednesday: Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, said Washington's answer to Iran's proposal was "not convincing" and that Hormuz would reopen only if the US met Iran's seven-point plan: lifting oil sanctions and the naval blockade, releasing frozen assets, a ceasefire on all fronts, compensation and guarantees against new attacks (Al Jazeera). Neither side has published the US text. Qatar's spokesman Majed al-Ansari says both sides "remain committed to the negotiation process," and Qatari mediators are pushing a draft with a three-day sequence of confidence-building steps (The Media Line). On Thursday Rezaei met Putin at the Kremlin and delivered a message from Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei; he repeated that Iran will neither open the strait nor negotiate until its conditions are met (Al Jazeera). In Washington, Trump told Fox "we have to do something large" but told the FT there's no immediate plan to strike, while saying the US is "locked and loaded." He acknowledged 18 US service members killed and called it "largely true" that the war cost Republicans the midterms; Vance said "the reason we lost is the war" (Gulf News). No new US strike has been confirmed since Qeshm. At sea, UKMTO confirmed a tanker hit by two projectiles about 50 nautical miles north of Qatar, and the shipping-security center JMIC counted four attacks in 96 hours; the IRGC claims several more tankers hit mines, which maritime authorities haven't confirmed. An Iranian lawmaker said ships from India, China, Iraq and Qatar are exempt from Iran's restrictions, with transit fees for Indian and Chinese vessels. Rep. Thomas Massie says he will force a House vote on impeaching Defense Secretary Hegseth over more than 90 days of unauthorized hostilities (Gulf News).

Why it matters

Both governments now read the US vote as a vote against the war, but they draw opposite conclusions: Tehran thinks time is on its side, and Washington hawks see two lame-duck months as their last window before a Democratic Congress.

How each side sees it

Tehran: the voters agree with us

Parliament speaker and chief negotiator Ghalibaf wrote that Americans said "NO TO WAR, and we endorse this message," and mocked US officials' "losing gambler energy." Rezaei's line is that the conditions come first, then the strait.

Via Gulf News (UAE) · Al Jazeera (Qatar-funded)
Restraint camp: the danger is the lame-duck window

Quincy's Trita Parsi argues "the pressure for war is now likely to increase," because hawks know the next two months are their last chance and an angry Trump may favor "vengeance."

Via Responsible Statecraft (restraint, cross-partisan)
Analysts on Iran's exemptions: a crack in the embargo

Lund University's Rouzbeh Parsi calls the exemptions "about poking holes in an embargo"; IG's Chris Beauchamp says they make "only limited difference at present" to oil flows.

Via Al Jazeera · The Media Line (Israel-based, center)

DevelopingIsraeli election · voted Oct 27

Eisenkot wins 67 recommendations, with both Arab-led lists behind him; Herzog decides Sunday

What happened

Since Wednesday: Herzog's consultations ended Thursday. 67 Knesset members recommended Gadi Eisenkot of Yashar: his own party, Bennett and Lapid's Together, Yisrael Beytenu, the Democrats, Ra'am (8 seats) and the Joint List (11). Netanyahu got 46. United Torah Judaism (7) recommended no one, the first time in six elections it hasn't backed Netanyahu (Times of Israel). Herzog said the results "appear clear" but pointed to "mutual vetoes" and said the real test is who can actually form a government; he'll announce Sunday. The nominee gets 28 days, extendable by 14. Joint List chair Yousef Jabareen called backing Eisenkot "a decision to choose between bad and worse," and said the list could support a minority government from outside (France 24). In his first appearance since the vote, Netanyahu warned against an Arab–Jewish coalition and alleged "serious suspicions" of foreign money in the election (Haaretz).

Why it matters

A government that depends on Arab parties, even from outside, would be a first for a prime minister from the security establishment. It also tests whether Lieberman, who has resisted such arrangements before, stays on board.

How each side sees it

The change bloc: the voters want something new

Yashar's Yoram Cohen says it would be "wrong to ignore the people's desire for change." Herzog told Likud's Miri Regev that "if you want to extend a hand," you can't call the other side a "disaster bloc."

Via Times of Israel (Israeli, center) · France 24 (French public)
Netanyahu's camp: an illegitimate coalition

Netanyahu and the nationalist right argue a government resting on Arab lists would be dangerous for the state, and Likud still disputes the election's integrity despite the committee's finding.

Via Haaretz (Israeli, left) · Arutz Sheva (Israeli, right/settler)

The trigger was talk of pausing the data-center build-out: Sen. Bernie Sanders posted "National data center moratorium now," Texas governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has promised a moratorium on approvals, and Wells Fargo estimates 27% of planned data-center gigawatts are in Texas (CNBC; Fortune). J.P. Morgan estimates 37.5 of the 96 gigawatts due in 2028 could face a pause. Bonds rallied: the 10-year fell to 4.84%, from 4.95% on election night.

Why it matters

NewFed / labor market · central banks

The Bank of England hikes to 4% on war inflation; Fed hike odds fall as jobs data lands today

What happened

The Bank of England voted 8–1 on Thursday to raise Bank Rate a quarter point to 4%, its first rise in more than three years; Catherine Mann wanted half a point. UK inflation was 3.5% in September and the bank now expects about 5% by the middle of next year (Bank of England minutes). Governor Andrew Bailey said "we've seen encouraging news in the past 48 hours," and the bank said future moves depend largely on the Iran war (AP via ADN). In the US, futures-implied odds of a December Fed hike fell to roughly 66–69% from about 83% Wednesday as stocks fell (Kitco). ADP said private employers added 86,000 jobs in October, weekly jobless claims fell, and summer productivity beat forecasts. Friday's jobs report is expected to show about 50,000 jobs and 4.2% unemployment (Continuum Economics).

Why it matters

Central banks are still fighting the oil shock even as oil falls. A weak jobs number today, after Thursday's rout, would make the Fed's December hike much harder to justify.

How each side sees it

Hawks: insure against inflation now

The BoE majority called the hike insurance against "upside risks" from stronger pass-through and firmer demand; Mann wanted to move faster.

Via Bank of England (primary)
Doves: the shock is fading, and so is growth

Continuum expects a "subdued report" pointing to slower growth in the fourth quarter, and traders are pricing a smaller chance of a Fed hike as oil falls and stocks slide.

Via Continuum Economics (macro research) · Kitco (metals trade)

What happened

Traders bet the midterm result would push Trump toward ending the war; Trump told Fox "I think it's the gasoline price" that cost Republicans. Heating-oil futures fell about 7% after the Energy Department loaned 300,000 barrels from the Northeast Home Heating Oil Reserve, nearly a third of it, to four companies (OilPrice.com). On Thursday Brent rebounded 3.9% to $98.29 and WTI 2.7% to $90.52 after new tanker attacks near Hormuz (Kitco). EIA's weekly report showed crude stocks down 0.9 million barrels to 409.8 million, 4% below the five-year average, and distillates down 3.6 million barrels, 13% below. Vortexa says Gulf exports averaged 17.1 million barrels a day in October, about three-quarters of pre-war levels. US natural gas rose to about $3.00 after a smaller-than-expected 77 Bcf storage build (Trading Economics). Gasoline averaged $4.12 Wednesday.

Why it matters

The war premium came out of oil on politics, not on new barrels. Distillate stocks are tight going into winter, so diesel stays the sore spot for freight and grocery prices.

Leo leaves for secular Uruguay, the first stop on a 12-day trip three weeks after lung surgery

What happened

Since Wednesday: Pope Leo left Rome's Fiumicino airport at 7:05 AM Friday on an ITA Airways flight to Montevideo, landing about 4:30 PM local. Day one: a welcome ceremony in Independence Square, President Yamandú Orsi, civil authorities and diplomats, then Vespers at the cathedral (Vatican News). It is his sixth foreign trip: about 12 days, 10 cities and nearly 40 addresses, all in Spanish, through Uruguay (Nov 6–8), Argentina (Nov 8–11, the first papal visit since 1987) and Peru (Nov 11–17). The Vatican says he is "in great health" with no lasting effects from the Oct 13 surgery, and the program includes meetings with abuse survivors and prisoners. Uruguay is about 38% Catholic and more than half unaffiliated, and baptisms fell from 41,673 in 1980 to 11,066 in 2024 (America). Tickets for the vigil at Centenario Stadium sold out in two days (AP via CP24). On Wednesday he received Bob Dylan, gave him a rosary and got a copy of Chronicles; Leo had quoted Dylan's "Go Away You Bomb" in a July preface on disarmament (Vatican News). After the midterms, Virginia's bishops said the state's new abortion-rights amendment will "embed virtually limitless abortion" in its constitution; Missouri's bishops, after voters rejected repealing abortion protections, said "this result... does not mark the end of our work" (EWTN).

Why it matters

The first American pope is going home to the region where he spent most of his ministry, after surgery and at 71, to the continent where the Church is losing ground fastest to secularism and evangelicals.

Week in review

Oct 30–Nov 6

The midterms. Democrats took both chambers, flipping at least seven Senate seats including Texas, Kansas and Maine, and leading for 250-plus House seats. Alaska and Mississippi are still counting. Johnson is stepping aside, and Republicans are arguing over whether the war or prices sank them.

Iran. The US struck Qeshm Island on Monday in a "defensive" raid, then the two sides traded replies through Qatar. Tehran says Hormuz stays closed until its seven conditions are met; Trump says he has no immediate strike plan.

Markets and energy. Brent went from about $118 last Friday to $94.62 Wednesday before bouncing to $98. Stocks had their best post-midterm day in decades, then their worst day since April 2025 as the data-center backlash hit AI shares. The Bank of England hiked to 4%.

Israel. Results were certified with no fraud found, and Eisenkot won 67 recommendations to Netanyahu's 46. Herzog decides Sunday.

Ukraine and the Gulf. The Oleshky truce ended, Russia hit Ukraine's grid hardest in weeks, and Abu Dhabi talks are set for Nov 10–11. On the Saudi front, missiles flew toward Mecca, Madinah and Najran, and the Houthis reportedly want $51 billion to stop.

Courts, Church and climate. The Supreme Court refused DOJ's bid to set aside California ballots and seemed ready to side with Colorado's Catholic preschools. Pope Leo, cleared after surgery, left for South America. COP31 opens Monday without a US delegation.

A vote against two booms

Tuesday's election was, by Time's count, the fifth time since World War II that one party flipped both chambers in a midterm. The usual reading is that voters punished a president over prices. The specific prices they cared about tell you where policy goes next. The two biggest stories in US life this year, the Iran war and the AI build-out, both turned up on the ballot, and both lost.

The war. The war that began Feb 28 has kept Hormuz largely closed, killed 18 US service members by Trump's own count and pushed gasoline to a record for an Election Day, about $4.15. The administration's own figures now blame it. Vance wrote that "the reason we lost is the war." Trump first blamed "the gasoline price," then called it "largely true" that the war cost Republicans, while saying he'd do it again. On the right, Townhall argues the economy, not the war, drove the result. Reason's Eric Boehm reached for 2006, when Iraq cost George W. Bush both chambers. AP's voter poll found about 7 in 10 voters wanted Congress to do more to check the president.

The data centers. The second rebuke was quieter until Thursday. Voters approved restrictions on data centers in about 40 local measures, half of them in Ohio, with a single exception. A CNBC voter poll found 56% of voters called AI and data centers a major factor. Democrats who ran against them won governorships in Texas, Georgia and Ohio. Texas matters most. Wells Fargo puts 27% of planned US data-center capacity there, and governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has promised a moratorium on approvals. Gov. Abbott's own September order had already halted permits pending a grid audit. Then Bernie Sanders posted "National data center moratorium now," and on Thursday about $3 trillion came off US stocks.

Why they're connected. Both stories are about who pays for scarce energy. The war took about a quarter of Gulf exports off the market and sent diesel above $6. The build-out is adding demand to grids that are already strained: data centers make up about 90% of new power requests on the Texas grid, Abbott's office says. Voters connected the two through their power and fuel bills. Heatmap's Robinson Meyer reads the new Democratic majority as "all of the above," focused on affordability and making data centers pay, not on climate as such.

The case that the market overreacted. J.P. Morgan's Michael Cembalest says three-quarters of 2027 data-center capacity is already being built and blames hedge-fund profit-taking for much of the selloff. A Democratic Congress can't pass a national moratorium over Trump's veto. Industry is already adapting: OpenAI's pledge to pay its own power costs is the kind of concession that could take the political heat out of local fights. Barclays sees a "war of attrition" in which building gets slower and costlier but doesn't stop, which favors the biggest balance sheets.

The case that it didn't. Moratoriums are mostly local and state decisions, not federal ones, so the veto doesn't protect against them. J.P. Morgan estimates 37.5 of the 96 gigawatts due in 2028 could face a pause. Meta's president says new US build-outs "will be very challenging." AI spending has carried a large share of US growth this year, so a slowdown is a macro risk, not just a problem for chip stocks.

And the war again. Here the vote may cut the other way. Quincy's Trita Parsi warns that the lame-duck months could bring more pressure for strikes, not less, because hawks know the window is closing. Tehran, reading the same result, has hardened its terms. Oil fell 9% on the hope of peace and rose 4% the next day on tanker attacks. Traders are pricing in a settlement before anyone has agreed to one.

What to watch. First, whether Trump treats the vote as a reason to end the war or to finish it before January; watch Qatar's three-day plan and CENTCOM's posture. Second, Hinojosa's first moves and whether other states copy Ohio's local measures, which would show whether "pay your own way" deals like OpenAI's can satisfy voters. Third, Nvidia's earnings later this month, the market's first hard data on whether demand is still running ahead of the politics.
