EscalatingIran war · day 238 (began Feb 28)

IRGC goes "offensive" and claims seven ships hit; Trump says talks are over

What happened

Since Wednesday: on Wednesday the Revolutionary Guard unveiled what it calls an "offensive and asymmetrical" doctrine. Spokesman Hossein Mohebi said that "if a country attacks us, we will both respond and launch an offensive." He claimed Iran's missile stockpile is 1.5 times its June 2025 size (Al Jazeera). On Thursday the IRGC Navy said it had hit seven ships. Four were struck during ship-to-ship oil transfers in the northern Gulf, and three hit mines west of Hormuz Wednesday night. It warned that the fight would extend to US bases if Washington keeps escorting tankers through. No ships were named and no casualties given; the claims are unverified, and we found no CENTCOM response (Tasnim, an IRGC-linked state outlet; Al-Monitor, citing Reuters). Trump said the US is no longer negotiating with Tehran and that a result will come "fairly soon." Rubio said Washington will back no deal that leaves Iran a path to a bomb. ABC reports US–Israeli strike planning for after the midterms (via Al Jazeera). Iran is still floating a seven-day plan to restore shipping in exchange for lifting the US port blockade, easing sanctions and unfreezing assets; enrichment remains the core dispute. CENTCOM says the blockade, resumed July 14, has diverted 70 ships. Bloomberg reports that crude flows through Hormuz have climbed back to pre-war levels with transponders switched off, while product shipments remain constrained (via Rigzone). We found nothing in the window on the UN Security Council meeting Iran requested or on Qatar's mediation.

Why it matters

Both sides are now describing the next round of fighting as a question of when, not whether. Trump's pledge of no strikes before Nov 3 still holds, which puts the danger point in the days right after the election.

How each side sees it

Tehran: enforcing our rules on the water

The IRGC presents the strikes as enforcement against "violating" and "illegal" oil transfers, and as a warning to the "aggressor" bases that support US escorts.

Via Tasnim (Iranian state-linked, IRGC-affiliated)
Restraint camp: war is coming back

Negar Mortazavi of the Center for International Policy says "another round of war seems to be approaching." She argues Tehran is raising the cost of renewed US strikes and trying to sway the midterms.

Via Al Jazeera (Qatar-funded; progressive analyst)
Arab analysts: Iran can't take a big new offensive

Rami Khouri of Arab Center Washington says "imminent conflict is on the cards." He expects strikes on Iranian infrastructure and doubts Iran could withstand a major new campaign.

Via Al Jazeera · Al-Monitor (Middle East news; Reuters wire)

NewVoting-law / executive-power rulings

Justices revive New Hampshire's student-ID ban for Nov 3; DOJ jumps to the 9th Circuit

What happened

On Thursday, in Scanlan v. Coalition for Open Democracy, the Supreme Court stayed a lower-court ruling that had barred New Hampshire from refusing student IDs as proof of identity for voting. The ban will apply on Nov 3. The unsigned order relied on the Purcell principle against changing election rules close to an election; Justice Jackson dissented (InDepthNH, Democracy Docket, docket 26A512). On Wednesday the Court denied, without explanation, the administration's emergency request to enforce its limits on gender-affirming care for transgender federal inmates; Justices Thomas and Alito would have granted it (NBC). In California, Judge David O. Carter let DOJ's noon Thursday deadline pass without ruling on its demand over about 20,000 ballots it says may belong to noncitizens. DOJ appealed to the 9th Circuit, calling his silence a "constructive denial"; California says DOJ lacks standing. NPR reports, by headline, that a federal judge restricted DHS field operations hunting for noncitizen voters. We found no D.C. Circuit ruling on the D.C. Guard.

How each side sees it

Voting-rights side: young voters lose

Plaintiffs' counsel at Free Speech For People called the stay wrong for student voters. Democracy Docket casts DOJ's California appeal as an attempt to get around a judge who hasn't ruled.

Via Democracy Docket (left, voting-rights) · Free Speech For People (plaintiffs' counsel)
State and DOJ side: settled rules, clean rolls

The law's sponsor, Rep. Donald MacFarlane, called the stay "a victory for common sense." On California, a federal prosecutor says DOJ has "verified 20,000 noncitizens" on the rolls, a claim California's attorney general calls meritless.

Via InDepthNH (NH nonprofit) · Fox News (right)
Watch: The 9th Circuit on California; the D.C. Guard ruling; the Nov 4 DHS ads hearing; Nov 2 arguments.

Iran. Mediation went nowhere. Lavrov and Pakistan's army chief visited Tehran, and hardliners turned them away. Araghchi said Hormuz stays shut "to everyone" until there is a deal, and the IRGC moved from mine claims to an "offensive" doctrine. By Thursday Trump said talks were over, and reports of post-election strike plans surfaced.

Yemen and Saudi Arabia. The deadliest week of the Houthi war: 73 killed at a government camp in Shabwa, missiles at Riyadh, and a deadly drone strike on Aramco's Shaybah field. Egypt delayed its vote on sending forces, Britain sent jets, and Washington told non-essential diplomats to leave the kingdom.

Ukraine. Trump waived most new Russia sanctions and lashed out at Zelensky, then sent Witkoff to Oslo and St. Petersburg. Moscow freed an American and agreed in principle to three-way talks, but kept pounding the grid and hit central Kyiv with cluster warheads.

Energy. Brent climbed from about $110 to $113, its highest settlement since early May. Diesel rose almost 10 cents to $6.28 with distillate stocks at multi-decade lows for the season, and the first Russian diesel cargo under the US waiver was due to sail. Natural gas fell to a two-month low.

Markets and the Fed. Housing data was weak and jobless claims stayed low. Thursday's AI-spending selloff, led by Meta and the chipmakers, pushed the 10-year below 5%. Markets expect a hold next week and a hike in December.

Elections. Texas set early-vote records, and three national polls put Trump's approval at record lows. Trump endorsed Netanyahu, whose bloc still trails. Brazil's runoff ended the week tied.

Courts, Church and AI. The Supreme Court took up race-neutral admissions and let New Hampshire's student-ID ban stand for Nov 3. Pope Leo returned to St. Peter's Square, needs no further treatment, and approved a major text on evangelization. In AI, a lone hacker's agents breached 100+ companies, AI-found PHP flaws rattled the web, and OpenAI and Anthropic set up an IPO race.

The countdown to Nov 4

For most of this autumn the Iran war has been held in a kind of suspension. Trump promised no new US strikes before the Nov 3 midterms, Iran squeezed shipping without closing the door to talks, and oil traders priced in a risk premium rather than a catastrophe. This week that suspension began to look like a countdown. On day 238 of the war, both Washington and Tehran are describing the next round of fighting as a matter of timing, and the timing points to the days just after Americans vote.

How we got here. The war began on Feb 28. A memorandum of understanding in June produced a pause, but talks stalled, and the US naval blockade of Iranian ports resumed July 14. CENTCOM says it has since diverted 70 ships. In early October the IRGC began enforcing what it calls a prohibited zone around Hormuz, attacking ships that run dark or take routes it hasn't approved. Two cargo ships sank last week. A second front opened in July when a Saudi-brokered truce with the Houthis collapsed; that war has since hit Riyadh's airport, Aden's presidential palace and, this week, Aramco's Shaybah field.

The diplomatic track has narrowed one door at a time. Iran offered a seven-day plan to reopen the strait in return for lifting the blockade, easing sanctions and unfreezing assets. Washington never answered it publicly. Russia's foreign minister went to Tehran on Oct 19 offering guarantees against renewed attacks and a Gulf security framework built around the UN Security Council's permanent members (Izvestia, pro-Kremlin). Pakistan's army chief followed with proposals that security chief Mohsen Rezaei rejected outright. Qatar says contacts never stopped. Then, on Thursday, Trump said the US is no longer negotiating and expects a result "fairly soon."

The case for pressure. Hawks, including ISW and US commanders, have argued for weeks that the blockade is working and that Iran's escalation at sea comes from weakness, not strength. On that reading, the answer to the new doctrine is more pressure, not concessions. Rubio has set the bar plainly: no deal that leaves Iran a path to a bomb. Netanyahu, campaigning for Tuesday's election, has gone further and pledged to bring the regime down. If his bloc holds on, Israeli pressure for post-election strikes will grow.

Tehran's case. Iran says it is enforcing its own waters against "illegal" oil transfers and answering a blockade that is itself an act of war. Its new doctrine, as the IRGC spokesman put it, is to "both respond and launch an offensive." Iranian officials have told Reuters they expect no breakthrough before the US vote and see the price of fuel as their best leverage on American voters.

The restraint view. Analysts such as Negar Mortazavi argue the pre-election freeze has only postponed a larger clash, with both sides using the time to build up. Rami Khouri of Arab Center Washington expects strikes on Iranian infrastructure and doubts Iran could withstand a major new offensive. The IRGC's own threat against US bases shows how such a round could widen. Gulf governments have paid the war's cost in lost exports and attacks on their cities, and Qatar has called for Hormuz to reopen without conditions.

What the market is saying. Brent settled at $113.17 Thursday, its highest since early May. The Brent–WTI gap near $14 shows traders pricing a threat specifically to seaborne Gulf barrels. Yet crude flows through Hormuz have, by Bloomberg's account, crept back toward pre-war levels with transponders switched off. Product shipments remain constrained, and US distillate stocks are about 9% below normal. The cushions are thin: the SPR sits near 283 million barrels, the lowest since 1982, and the IEA's coordinated releases are mostly spent.

What to watch. First, Tuesday's Israeli vote and how quickly a coalition forms. Second, whether the IRGC follows through on its threat against US bases, which would end the pre-election freeze on its own terms. Third, the departure orders. Washington has so far told diplomats to leave Saudi Arabia only; a wider Gulf drawdown would be the clearest signal that the countdown is real.
