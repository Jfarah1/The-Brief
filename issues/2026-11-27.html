DevelopingFederal death penalty push

The death-penalty memorandum is now in the Federal Register

What happened

Since Wednesday: Trump's Nov 23 memorandum on "Expeditious Enforcement of Capital Sentences" was published in the Federal Register today. It tells the attorney general to carry out federal death sentences "as expeditiously as possible." It also tells DOJ to take federal jurisdiction and seek death in murders of minors and in murders tied to rape, trafficking or kidnapping. It covers federal cases only. The only other presidential action in the window was the Thanksgiving proclamation (whitehouse.gov).

Why it matters

Publication makes the directive formal federal policy. The test will be DOJ's capital notices in new cases and how fast courts move on appeals.

Via Conservative Daily News (right)

Via The Boston Report (left-leaning)

NewUS–China–Russia summitry · trade

The trade office proposes 15–35% tariffs on 14 economies; partners push back

What happened

Since Wednesday: on Nov 25 USTR found "structural excess capacity" in 14 of the 16 economies it has investigated since March and proposed new Section 301 tariffs. Mexico and Norway were deferred (USTR). The proposed maximum rates: Vietnam 35%, India 25%, China and Singapore 20%, five Southeast Asian economies 18%, and the EU, Japan, Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan 15%. For China the 20% is net of the forced-labor 301 rate, some goods get 10%, and the "30-for-30" product list is exempt (KPMG). Holland & Knight adds that goods already under Section 232, patented drugs and semiconductors are excluded. Comments are due Jan 5, hearings are Jan 25, and nothing takes effect before then. Beijing called the probe "a typical act of unilateralism" and "reserves the right to take necessary measures" (Global Times, Chinese state media). Vietnam, Japan, Korea, the EU and India objected (Hans India).

Why it matters

This is the administration's legal rebuild of the tariff wall the Supreme Court tore down, now on a statute built for it. It lands six weeks before the US–China truce expires. See the long read.

How each side sees it

USTR and domestic steel: overcapacity is real

USTR defines the target as production capacity that "exceeds domestic and global demand." The American Iron and Steel Institute urged cumulative tariffs on the "worst actors" at the May hearings, and Steel Market Update reports it now says the proposals should go further.

Via USTR (primary) · SteelOrbis (steel trade) · Steel Market Update (steel trade)
Trading partners: a false premise

China's Foreign Ministry calls "overcapacity" "a false proposition." Vietnam's deputy PM says the rate is "not consistent" with relations between the two countries. Korea and Japan expect existing deals to be honored.

Via Global Times (Chinese state media) · Hans India (Indian)
US free-traders: bad economics, strained law

The Progressive Policy Institute's Ed Gresser told the 301 committee in May that the probe is "inconsistent with statutory goals" and misunderstands economics.

Via Progressive Policy Institute (center-left)

The tariff wall, rebuilt: what the Section 301 proposal does and where it goes next

For most of this year, the president's tariffs have been on borrowed time. In February the Supreme Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act didn't let the president impose sweeping import duties. The administration bridged the gap with temporary tariffs under Section 122 of the Trade Act, a balance-of-payments provision capped at 150 days, which ran out July 24. In March it opened Section 301 investigations of 16 economies for what it calls "structural excess capacity." On Wednesday, the day before Thanksgiving, it delivered the results.

What the law allows. Section 301 lets the US Trade Representative act against foreign practices that are "unreasonable" and burden US commerce. It's the statute behind the 2018 China tariffs, and courts have generally upheld its use when the procedure is followed: an investigation, findings, public comment and hearings. That process is the point. The emergency-powers theory let the president move overnight. Section 301 is slower but much harder to challenge in court. The administration is trading speed for durability.

What it proposes. Rates are ceilings, set country by country: Vietnam up to 35%, India 25%, China and Singapore 20%, Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand 18%, and the EU, Japan, Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan 15% (KPMG). The 15% tier matches the caps in this year's bilateral deals, which suggests USTR is turning negotiated terms into law rather than raising them. China's treatment is the most engineered. Its 20% is net of the existing forced-labor duty, some goods get 10%, and the "30-for-30" list agreed at the leaders' level is exempt. Goods already covered by Section 232 national-security tariffs, patented drugs and semiconductors are excluded (Holland & Knight). Mexico and Norway were deferred.

The case for it. The administration's argument is that some economies, led by China, build capacity well beyond what their own markets or the world can absorb, and dump the surplus abroad. USTR's definition is capacity that "exceeds domestic and global demand." That is a real concern well beyond this White House. The steel industry has made the argument for decades, and the American Iron and Steel Institute told the May hearings to stack tariffs on the "worst actors." It reportedly thinks this week's proposal is too mild. Supporters also argue a statute-based system gives partners and importers more predictability than emergency orders did.

The case against it. Critics say the concept is elastic. Ed Gresser of the Progressive Policy Institute told the 301 committee the probe is "inconsistent with statutory goals" and misreads economics. He argues that a trade deficit or a large export sector isn't evidence of an unfair practice. Applying one finding to 14 very different economies, from Switzerland to Bangladesh, invites the charge that 301 is being used as a general tariff authority by another name. That's the kind of argument that sank the emergency-powers theory. Abroad, the reaction was uniform. Beijing called it "unilateralism" and overcapacity "a false proposition" (Global Times, state media). Vietnam said its 35% rate is "not consistent" with the two countries' relationship, and Japan, Korea, the EU and India objected (Hans India).

The China clock. The proposal lands inside a truce. China has suspended its extra 24% tariff, keeping 10%, and paused port fees and export controls until Jan 10. It said explicitly that it would watch how the US replaced its emergency tariffs and "adjust" (Baker McKenzie; SCMP). The 20% ceiling and the 30-for-30 carve-out look designed to stay inside that bargain. The comment deadline of Jan 5 and hearings on Jan 25 straddle the truce's expiry, giving both sides leverage and a reason to wait.

Congress's role. None directly: Section 301 is delegated authority, and no vote is required. But the incoming Democratic Congress can hold hearings, and tariff revenue is now built into budget math in the funding fight. Any lawsuit would likely focus on whether "excess capacity" is a practice Section 301 can reach.

What to watch. Whether Beijing names countermeasures before Jan 10. Whether the 15% partners get carve-outs in the final notice. Whether Vietnam and India strike deals at the lower rates USTR has signaled (20% and 17%, respectively, per trade-law summaries). And the first court challenge, which will test whether the administration has finally found a tariff authority that holds up.
