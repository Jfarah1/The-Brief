EscalatingSaudi–Houthi war · truce collapsed in July

Houthis hit Aramco in Dhahran; Saudi jets strike Yemen's fuel sites

What happened

Since Monday: on Tuesday a Houthi missile hit an Aramco administrative building in Dhahran. The Saudi Energy Ministry says it caused a "limited fire" and no casualties. Debris from missiles intercepted over Riyadh injured four people and damaged 15 homes, and King Khalid airport will stay shut through Oct 17 (CBS). The death toll from Saturday's airport strike now stands at 13, including one American, with more than 300 wounded (CBS, Al Jazeera). The same day, the Saudi-led coalition told civilians to leave, then struck the Yemen Oil Company's al-Sabaha station in Sanaa and fuel sites in Hodeidah, Ras Isa and Dhamar. The Houthi-run health ministry reported two dead at the Hodeidah site; the oil company said Sabaha's tanks were empty. Houthi media counted 104 Saudi strikes in 24 hours (Al Jazeera, France 24). Trump still won't say whether the US will join. He wrote on Truth Social that the Houthis are "behaving very badly," probably "at the behest of Iran," and said Turkey's Erdogan "would do as I ask" to support the Saudis.

Why it matters

The Houthis have now hit Saudi energy infrastructure, not just airports, which puts the world's largest crude exporter's output in play with Hormuz flows already well below normal. Both sides are now targeting fuel infrastructure, and the US decision remains open.

How each side sees it

Riyadh: consequences are coming

The Saudi cabinet said the Houthis will face "consequences," and the Energy Minister said the response "must be equally serious." Riyadh pledged to keep exports flowing.

Via Al Jazeera (Qatar-funded) · CBS News
Sanaa: we will answer in Riyadh

Spokesman Yahya Saree threatened a "harsh and painful" response in Riyadh if Sanaa is struck. Official Mohammed al-Bukhaiti warned of a "swift and painful" response against US interests if Washington joins.

Via France 24 (French public broadcaster) · CBS News
Restraint camp: Riyadh's strategy has failed

Responsible Statecraft argued last month that Saudi Arabia's anti-Houthi strategy has "crashed and burned" and that Riyadh may end up negotiating on Houthi terms. That piece predates this week's strikes.

Via Responsible Statecraft (restraint, cross-partisan)
Watch: Whether the US joins strikes; any Aramco output impact; Riyadh airport reopening Oct 17.

DevelopingIran war · day 229 (began Feb 28)

IRGC claims a third tanker in two days; no new US offer

What happened

Since Monday: on Monday UKMTO reported a tanker hit by a projectile while leaving Hormuz, and the IRGC claimed it as its third ship in two days. There have been at least 18 attacks on ships around the strait since Oct 1 (Democracy Now). Security chief Mohsen Rezaei repeated that Tehran has stopped responding to Washington, saying the US accepted some of Iran's seven conditions while others remain unclear, and accused CENTCOM's commander of overseeing "oil-smuggling operations" (Ahram Online). On Tuesday IRGC Navy chief Azmaei said Iran is waiting for US warships to approach "so that we can sink them." CENTCOM replied that the "IRGC's navy commander has no navy" (Fox). At rallies Trump said "wait until you see what we do to Iran," and also predicted the war would end soon. We found no report of a new US counter-offer. A CBS report that Iran's foreign ministry says it is still "reviewing" the US reply could not be dated with confidence.

Why it matters

Diplomacy is stalled, and the shipping war is doing the talking. Kpler put crude flows through the strait just under 10M barrels a day as of Oct 7, down from about 15.5M in September (OilPrice, Maritime Executive).

How each side sees it

Tehran: the US reply falls short

Rezaei says Washington's answer is insufficient; Iranian officials have also threatened regional data cables if fighting resumes.

Via Ahram Online (Egyptian state-owned)
Washington: no navy to fear

CENTCOM dismisses the IRGC threats, and Trump hints at more force. The hawkish Institute for the Study of War assesses that Iran is now targeting ship-to-ship oil transfers.

Via Fox News (right) · Democracy Now (left; pairs Fox)
Watch: A US counter-offer; more tanker strikes; no US strikes on Iran pledged before Nov 3.

EscalatingUkraine war / peace track

The "energy ceasefire" fails its first test

What happened

Since Monday: Russian strikes on Ukrainian energy targets continued for three straight nights. Overnight into Wednesday, Zelensky said Russia fired 39 ballistic missiles and more than 120 drones at Kyiv, the Kyiv region and Kharkiv. At least 600,000 people lost power and five were killed in Kyiv. Russia's Defense Ministry said it hit six power plants (AP). Ukraine says it has paused its own refinery strikes; its last was Oct 9. On Monday Lavrov said of Zelensky: "I do not think he deserves a respite." Peskov said contacts are "ongoing" and rejected Kyiv's proposal for a 60-day truce to allow elections (Ukrainska Pravda). Trump insists "they agreed" and says there were "violations on both sides" (Al Jazeera). Zelensky is in London today with Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who said Britain is with Ukraine "for as long as it takes." Separately, Ukrainian officials say Starlink has throttled terminals mounted on drones; adviser Serhii Beskrestnov said "this action cannot be a coincidence." SpaceX did not comment (AP).

Why it matters

The ceasefire was the political cover for the Russian diesel waiver (see Oil & Gas). If it collapses, so does much of the case for the waiver, just as winter heating demand begins.

How each side sees it

Kyiv: Moscow is cashing in

Zelensky: "This is what happens when Russia sees the prospect of higher revenues for its war machine." Ukrainska Pravda reports Moscow is rejecting the truce outright.

Via Ukrainska Pravda (Ukrainian) · AP via WSLS
Moscow: no favors for Zelensky

Lavrov and Peskov frame any pause as something to be earned in a wider settlement. The Kremlin says talks continue but treats Kyiv's election truce as a non-starter.

Via Ukrainska Pravda (Ukrainian, reporting Kremlin remarks) · Al Jazeera (Qatar-funded)
⚠ We couldn't reach in-window Russian outlets (TASS, Meduza, Moscow Times); the Kremlin view here is as relayed by others.

Via Al Jazeera (Qatar-funded)

DevelopingFed tightening cycle

Inflation runs hotter than expected: 3.7% headline, core up 0.3%

What happened

This morning: consumer prices rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% from a year earlier, up from 3.4% in August and above the 3.6% forecast. Core prices (excluding food and energy) rose 0.3% for a second month, to 2.6% a year, against forecasts of 0.2% and 2.5%. Energy rose 5.2% in the month and gasoline 9.1%; gasoline is up 34.2% from a year ago (BLS). Since Monday, New York Fed President John Williams said one more hike may be needed this year but likely none in 2027; Governor Waller called inflation expectations "well anchored." Market pricing cited by Trading Economics puts about a 77% chance on a hold at the Oct 27–28 meeting and about 88% on a December hike. The 10-year eased to 5.21% Tuesday, off its Oct 7 intraday high of 5.364%, the highest since 2002. Bank earnings began: JPMorgan reported third-quarter profit of $15.3B ($5.57 a share), up from $14.4B a year ago (SEC filing). The S&P was flat Tuesday as several large banks fell after results.

How each side sees it

Markets: it's mostly gasoline

Before the release, MUFG said another upside surprise in core "would likely be needed" to revive bets on an October hike, and today's core reading was just that. Franklin Templeton's Schulze, quoted by OilPrice, says moderation "may already be underway" even with oil above $90.

Via investingLive (markets news) · OilPrice (energy trade)
Main Street: people feel energy first

Gasoline is up 7.1% in a month. Economist Sung Won Sohn: "People care about food and energy. That's what they feel every day." Oil costs also take time to pass through to other prices.

Via OilPrice (energy trade) · Trading Economics (data)
Watch: PPI Thursday; Warsh at the IMF Thursday; blackout from Saturday; FOMC Oct 27–28.

DevelopingEnergy / diesel crisis · since March

Backlash builds over Russian diesel as the IEA weighs faster releases

What happened

Since Monday: seven senators from both parties (Collins, Tillis, Curtis, Murkowski, Shaheen, Blumenthal, Wyden) urged Trump to reverse the waiver letting Russian diesel in through April 2027 (OFAC General License 135); Sen. Jerry Moran added his criticism Monday. Trump dismissed critics as lawmakers who "can't really be serious." No deliveries of the promised 300,000 tonnes are confirmed. In Monday night's Iowa Senate debate, both Republican Ashley Hinson and Democrat Josh Turek opposed the deal (Washington Examiner). Ahead of Thursday's IEA board meeting, chief Fatih Birol asked members to speed up what's left of March's 400M-barrel pledge, diesel first; 325M has been released. Germany reportedly plans no new release, and European officials say the G7's 100M-barrel figure mostly repackages old pledges (Euronews). EIA's weekly diesel price, delayed by the holiday, is due today.

Why it matters

Diesel moves freight and farm costs, and it's the price the White House most wants down before Nov 3. With the energy truce failing, the political trade behind the waiver is harder to defend.

How each side sees it

White House: relief at the pump

The administration presents the deal as quick supply relief for truckers and farmers ahead of winter, and Trump says the US will import 300,000 tonnes immediately.

Via OilPrice / RFE-RL (energy trade; US-funded broadcaster)
Critics: leverage traded for little fuel

The Atlantic Council's Mark Temnycky doubts Russia can deliver; the first tranche is about half a day of US use. The bipartisan senators say it hands Moscow revenue mid-war.

Via OilPrice / RFE-RL · Washington Examiner (right) · Al Jazeera
Watch: EIA diesel today; IEA Thursday; a reported House GOP move to block the deal (headline only, unverified).

DevelopingSupply picture

OPEC sees a 2027 rebound; Gulf output returns after Isaias

What happened

OPEC's monthly report on Tuesday cut 2026 demand growth to about 200,000 barrels a day but raised 2027 growth to 2.6M b/d, taking demand to 108.3M b/d. It put OECD commercial stocks 177.2M barrels below the 2015–19 average (OilPrice). In the Gulf of Mexico, about 1.17M b/d of oil was still offline Sunday per the Marine Minerals Administration, down from a peak near 1.5M; BP and Shell are returning crews and Shell reports no damage (Argus). We found no newer shut-in figure. DOE approved a 4M-barrel hurricane exchange from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve on Oct 10 (2M each to ExxonMobil and BP). The reserve stands at 283.8M barrels, the lowest since 1982.

How each side sees it

Producers: demand will roar back

OPEC's numbers argue the market will need more oil in 2027, not less, and that today's thin inventories will tighten further.

Via OilPrice (energy trade)
Consumers' agency: release faster now

The IEA's push to accelerate stock releases reflects the opposite worry: that supply, not demand, is today's problem, and that governments are slow to act.

Via Euronews (European) · Argus (energy pricing) · DOE (primary)
Watch: EIA weekly report Thursday; Gulf restart figures; OPEC+ Nov 1.

NewSCOTUS October Term 2026

Justices take up DOGE's records; ad-rate fight still pending

What happened

Monday's order list granted review in U.S. DOGE Service v. U.S. District Court, the watchdog group CREW's fight to get DOGE's records under the Freedom of Information Act, plus two other cases. It also sent Monsanto v. Dennis back for reconsideration in light of Durnell (Supreme Court order list; AP/PBS). Tuesday the Court heard Beaird, on whether judges still defer to Sentencing Commission commentary; today it hears Salazar v. Paramount, on whether a 1988 video-rental privacy law covers a newsletter subscriber tracked by a Meta pixel (SCOTUSblog, Courthouse News). Via PBS NewsHour / AP · Order list (primary)
CREW: it acted like an agency

CREW says DOGE exercised real authority over agencies and so is covered by FOIA. On ad rates, the Campaign Legal Center urges the Court to deny the GOP application.

Via PBS NewsHour / AP · Campaign Legal Center (left-leaning)

NewPontificate of Leo XIV · Amoris review, final day

Pope Leo has lung cancer; surgery went without complications

What happened

Since Monday: Leo entered Rome's Gemelli hospital Tuesday, and surgeons removed the lower lobe of his right lung with a minimally invasive, video-assisted technique. The Vatican says there were no complications and that a biopsy during the operation "confirmed the diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma," which doctors had suspected beforehand. Further tests on the tissue will come "in the coming days," and the one-week stay is unchanged. This morning the Vatican said "the night passed calmly." Stage, prognosis and any further treatment have not been disclosed (Vatican News, The Pillar). Public events are cancelled through Oct 20, and his Nov 6–17 trip to South America is in doubt. Church law has no provision for a sick or incapacitated pope (America/AP). At the Amoris Laetitia meeting, organizers said the synthesis report won't be published as planned; it goes to Leo after he recovers. Cardinal Tagle presided at today's closing Mass and read the Pope's homily, which urged the Church to take the document "to heart." Separately, Archbishop Broglio of the US military archdiocese said Catholic soldiers should ask to be excused from firing-squad duty, as the US plans to livestream an execution.

Why it matters

A cancer diagnosis 17 months into a pontificate raises questions about Leo's schedule, his travel and his health, and how much the Vatican will say about it. It also delays his own verdict on the Amoris review.

How each side sees it

Progressive: accompaniment and "fragility"

America reports Cardinal Tobin saying Communion for the divorced and remarried is "one of the ways" fragility was addressed. USCCB president Archbishop Coakley said it hadn't come up at his table. Synod official Fr. Giacomo Costa: "The primary question is not whether or not people are cohabiting."

Via America (center-left, Jesuit)
Center-right: move on from old battles

Patrick T. Brown of the Ethics and Public Policy Center writes that the document was "trapped fighting battles that turned the Church inward," and urges a focus on falling marriage rates.

Via National Catholic Register (conservative)
Traditionalist: a closed process

LifeSiteNews calls the unpublished report a sign of secrecy and says the agenda was tightly controlled. It also objected to one speaker's remarks on remarriage; America's account of the same remarks differs.

Via LifeSiteNews (traditionalist)
Watch: Pathology results and any word on further treatment; the South America trip; Leo's own reflections on Amoris.
