EscalatingIran war · day 250 (began Feb 28)

The US hits Qeshm Island in a "defensive" strike as Iran's negotiators leave Doha and Tehran sends a reply

What happened

Since Monday: US aircraft struck Qeshm Island, inside the Strait of Hormuz, late Monday. A US official told reporters the strikes were limited and "defensive," a response to Iranians "using surface-to-air missiles against US assets," and not a resumption of major combat (Times of Israel). Euronews, citing US media, says the target was an IRGC surface-to-air launcher; CENTCOM issued no statement. The IRGC said it downed two US reconnaissance drones and an MQ-9. IRNA reported five explosions on the island, which hosts an underground missile site, a naval base and a desalination plant. Iran International, citing the WSJ, says Qeshm was one of several limited US strikes carried out unannounced in recent weeks. On diplomacy: Iran's delegation left Doha on Sunday without a breakthrough, though a draft was still circulating, and Qatari mediators went to Tehran Monday night. Foreign Minister Araghchi says Hormuz could reopen "within two days" if Washington accepts Iran's conditions; President Pezeshkian says Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei gave the government "certain authorities" over talks. On Wednesday Ghalibaf, now described as Iran's chief negotiator, said Tehran had given Washington a "rational response" (Times of Israel); its contents aren't public. Trump, asked twice whether he'd strike after the midterms, said "No," and posted that "Iran wants a settlement, as soon as possible." Vance: "We've certainly made some progress." Oil fell 7.6% Monday on the talk of a deal.

Why it matters

The strike window Gulf officials pointed to for today has turned into a strike-and-talk pattern instead: small US hits around the strait while both sides trade drafts. A Democratic Congress from January adds pressure on the White House to settle or seek authorization.

How each side sees it

Tehran: the ball is in America's court

Parliament speaker Ghalibaf warns any US "strategic error" would open "a new battlefield" and says Iran is at maximum readiness. Tehran's Hamshahri reports Iran's target list now includes US support bases in Europe. After the US vote, Ghalibaf wrote that "Americans said NO TO WAR."

Via Euronews (European) · Al Jazeera (Qatar-funded)
Washington: self-defense, and a deal is close

US officials frame Qeshm as protecting shipping, not a new campaign. Trump says "We had a war with Iran. We won," and both he and Vance sound upbeat about a settlement. Reports say Netanyahu has been pressing Trump to resume large-scale strikes.

Via Times of Israel (Israeli, center) · Iran International (diaspora, anti-regime; citing WSJ)

DevelopingIsraeli election · voted Oct 27

Results certified with "no clear winner"; Herzog's consultations begin and Eisenkot is narrowly ahead on recommendations

What happened

Since Monday: Central Elections Committee chair Noam Sohlberg handed President Herzog the official results Wednesday, unchanged: Netanyahu's bloc 53, Eisenkot's bloc 48, Arab parties 19 (Likud 27, Yashar 21). The committee found no evidence the vote was compromised, rejecting fraud claims from Likud; Herzog said there was "no clear winner" and that suspicions of foreign interference should go to the Shin Bet and police (i24NEWS). Consultations with factions start Wednesday evening Israel time, smallest parties first. Ynet's count of expected recommendations: Eisenkot 48 (Yashar, Together, the Democrats, Yisrael Beiteinu), Netanyahu 46 (Likud, Otzma Yehudit, Shas, Religious Zionism), with UTJ, Ra'am and the Joint List undeclared. Lieberman backs Eisenkot and rules out both a Netanyahu coalition and a minority government (Middle East Monitor), though Ynet reports a tense meeting where he accused Eisenkot of damaging the bloc. Herzog urged leaders to "stop climbing trees," which the Times of Israel reads as a push for unity. The Knesset is sworn in Nov 10.

Why it matters

Whoever gets the first mandate still lacks a path to 61 without a partner the other side has vetoed. The undeclared Arab and ultra-Orthodox parties now decide who goes first.

How each side sees it

The opposition: Netanyahu is the obstacle

Opposition figures read the US result as a sign of "just how toxic Netanyahu has become" (Yair Golan), and Lapid says it rules out a Netanyahu-led unity government.

Via Al Jazeera · Middle East Monitor (UK-based, pro-Palestinian)

Likud alleged fraud before certification and still leads the largest bloc. The committee rejected the fraud claims; Netanyahu made no immediate public comment on certification or the US vote.

EscalatingUkraine war / peace track

The Oleshky truce lapses, Russia hits the grid and an aid line, and Abu Dhabi talks are set for Nov 10–11

What happened

Since Monday: the six-day Oleshky truce ended Sunday night and wasn't extended; Ukraine's General Staff says 167 civilians were evacuated. Zelensky says Kyiv offered to extend it and Russia answered with strikes. On Monday a double strike hit a school in Komyshuvakha, Zaporizhzhia region, where aid was being handed out; France 24 counted at least 26 dead, later reports 31. On Tuesday Ukrenergo called Russia's attack on more than 20 energy sites one of the largest of the war; nuclear plants cut output and Kyiv lost power and water (Kyiv Post; UNN). Overnight Monday drones set a major fire at the Slavneft-YANOS refinery in Yaroslavl, injuring two workers (Meduza; Moscow Times); Kyiv hasn't claimed it. The Kremlin and envoy Steve Witkoff confirmed three-way US–Russia–Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi on Nov 10–11, the first since February, with Medinsky leading for Russia and Witkoff and Kushner for the US (Moscow Times; TASS, state media).

Why it matters

The truce was the test of whether local pauses could build toward talks; it ended in some of the war's heaviest strikes in weeks. Abu Dhabi is now the next marker, and oil traders are watching it for any easing on Russian supply.

How each side sees it

Kyiv: we offered more, Moscow answered with missiles

Via Kyiv Post (Ukrainian) · France 24 (French public)

DevelopingEnergy / diesel crisis

Oil's biggest one-day drop since July on Iran deal hopes; diesel edges down from near-record

What happened

Since Monday: Brent settled at $104.77 on Monday, down 7.6%, and WTI at $96.46, down 7.3%, the biggest one-day percentage drop since July 27, after Iranian reports that the government had authority to seek talks and Trump's "No" on post-election strikes (NBC; Globe and Mail). Diesel futures fell 5.4%. On Tuesday Brent eased to $103.60, a four-week low, and WTI to $96.09 (Capp Notes; Nation Thailand). EIA's weekly retail diesel price fell 6 cents to $6.292 a gallon, and regular gasoline 11.6 cents to $4.153 (EIA). Gas still averaged a record for an Election Day. The war's supply loss hasn't gone away: OPEC+'s seven core members held December quotas at 31.01 million barrels a day, about 4.2 million above what they're actually pumping, and Iran threatened to seize ships of South Korea's Sinokor from Nov 1 (OilPrice.com). Aramco says it is expanding its East-West pipeline, which bypasses Hormuz, from 7 to 10 million barrels a day by 2029.

Why it matters

The drop is a bet on diplomacy, not on barrels: no new oil has moved through the strait. If Iran's reply or a Qeshm-style incident disappoints, the $8 risk premium can come back as fast as it left.

How each side sees it

Bulls on price: the shortage is still real

Goldman raised its December Brent forecast to $105 and sees $140 if Gulf output stays 3.5 million barrels a day below 2025 levels. Freight from inside the Gulf remains "prohibitively high."

Via OilPrice.com (energy trade)
Bears: the curve is softening

Steep backwardation is flattening, with Dubai's near-term spread near zero, a sign Asian buyers are less desperate. ING still warns escalation risk "has not passed."

Via OilPrice.com · Nation Thailand (Thai; wire copy) · Oil & Gas Middle East (energy trade)

NewUS midterms · results

Democrats take the Senate and the House, flipping Texas, Kansas, Iowa, Ohio, North Carolina, South Carolina and Maine

What happened

AP called the Senate for Democrats overnight, and the House at about 1:25 AM ET (Time). Democrats flipped at least seven Senate seats: Texas (James Talarico over Ken Paxton, the state's first Democratic senator elected since 1988), Kansas (Adam Hamilton, the first Kansas Democrat since 1932), Iowa (Josh Turek), Ohio (Sherrod Brown), North Carolina (Roy Cooper), South Carolina (over appointed Sen. Darline Graham) and Maine, where Troy Jackson beat Susan Collins, who conceded (CNBC; Time). Democrats held Georgia (Ossoff), Michigan (Abdul El-Sayed, who becomes the first Muslim senator), Minnesota and New Hampshire, where Chris Pappas becomes the first openly gay man elected to the Senate. Republicans held Florida (Ashley Moody) and Nebraska. Alaska (Sullivan vs. Peltola) and Mississippi (Hyde-Smith vs. Colom) were still uncalled at last check (CBS). Outlets' running Senate totals differ, from 51 to 54 Democratic seats, so we aren't giving a final number. In the House, about 75 races were uncalled early Wednesday; CNBC's election desk projects at least 245 Democratic seats. Democrats also flipped governorships in Texas (Gina Hinojosa over Greg Abbott), Ohio (Amy Acton over Vivek Ramaswamy) and Georgia (Keisha Lance Bottoms). Time notes this is only the fifth time since World War II a party has flipped both chambers in a midterm. Gas averaged a record for an Election Day, about $4.15 (AAA, via Time).

Why it matters

From January, Democrats can block nominees, open investigations and set terms on war funding and tariffs for the last two years of Trump's term. AP's voter poll found about 7 in 10 voters, including about 4 in 10 Republicans, saying Congress isn't doing enough to check the president.

How each side sees it

Hakeem Jeffries: "Today begins the great American comeback." Talarico said "all the money in the world is no match for the people of Texas," aimed at Elon Musk's $150 million-plus for pro-Paxton PACs (Breitbart). Rep. Nikki Budzinski called the result a "repudiation" of Trump's policies.

Via Time (newsweekly, center) · CNBC (mainstream)

Conservatives blamed the war and prices. The Daily Wire's Matt Walsh: "Trump launched an unpopular and pointless war." Buck Sexton cited "poor candidates" and "high prices"; Erick Erickson listed tariffs, corruption and redistricting too. Thune vowed Senate Republicans "will not stand idly by."

Via Fox News (mainstream, right) · Breitbart (right)
Trump: not about me

Trump said Democrats "did a good job," that he doesn't "view it as a hit on me," and that he is "absolutely willing to accept those results." He blamed gas prices and posted that "we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE" had he been on the ballot. He called Abbott a "RINO."

Via Time · AP Voter Poll (wire, via WDIV)

DevelopingSCOTUS October Term 2026 · November sitting

The justices look ready to side with Colorado's Catholic preschools, but probably without overruling Smith

What happened

Since Monday: at Tuesday's argument in St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, a majority seemed poised to rule for the parishes, SCOTUSblog's Amy Howe reports, though no justice called for overruling Employment Division v. Smith; likelier routes are applying strict scrutiny or sending the case back. Justice Barrett asked why Colorado can't expressly exclude religious schools yet can impose a condition they "will be unable to agree to," citing an op-ed urging states to blunt Carson v. Makin this way. Kavanaugh, noting more than 2,000 participating preschools, asked why the state wouldn't "seek the win/win." Gorsuch asked what would be left of Sherbert if everything turned on express discrimination, and Roberts said Maine's law in Carson had "pretty much the same" effect. Kagan said the earlier cases involved laws singling out religious schools, while Colorado is enforcing "its own nondiscrimination principles"; Sotomayor warned of "a great cost to someone" either way, and Jackson said the schools "want to discriminate by exclusion." Becket's Nick Reaves argued for the parish, Colorado's Helen Norton for the state, and the administration's Sarah Harris offered a narrower path. Monday's two cases were technical: Maxwell v. Dinis (habeas and halfway-house transfers, possibly moot by January) and Younge v. Fulton (unpleaded defenses).

Why it matters

A ruling for the parishes would extend the Carson line from laws that name religion to neutral-sounding conditions that religious providers can't meet, affecting voucher, preschool and grant programs nationwide.

How each side sees it

The parishes: faith shouldn't cost public benefits

Reaves: "Americans don't have to forfeit their faith to receive public benefits." First Liberty's Kelly Shackelford says excluding the schools "is wrong," and the Register's commentary argues Colorado engineered the exclusion.

Via Daily Caller (right) · National Catholic Register (conservative) · First Liberty (religious-liberty litigator)
Colorado's side: open doors for every family

Lambda Legal's Karen Loewy: "the schoolhouse door must be open to every family." COLAGE's Meg York says a parish win isn't "a foregone conclusion." Kagan asked, "Why would we put ourselves in the middle of that?"

Via Washington Blade (LGBTQ, left) · Mother Jones (left)
Court-watchers: a narrow win is the likeliest result

SCOTUSblog reads the bench as favoring the parishes on a narrow theory; Chalkbeat quotes Kavanaugh: "Just trying to figure out why we're here."

Via SCOTUSblog (nonpartisan) · Chalkbeat (education nonprofit)

DevelopingVoting-law / executive-power rulings

The Supreme Court turns away DOJ's bid to set aside 20,000 California ballots, without a noted dissent

What happened

Since Monday: DOJ filed its emergency application on Monday, and the Court denied it hours later with no noted dissents and without asking California to respond (SCOTUSblog; CalMatters). Justice Kagan had referred it to the full Court. In Alaska, DOJ's appeal of Judge Gleason's dismissal was pending at the Ninth Circuit with no action as of midday Tuesday (The Alaska Story), and the Senate race there is still uncalled. In a separate case on threats to prosecute election officials in California and Washington, an 11-judge Ninth Circuit en banc panel voted 6–5 Tuesday to reinstate a stay of Judge Trina Thompson's order protecting them; plaintiffs sought a full-court rehearing (Democracy Docket). On Monday night Judge Reggie Walton declined to bar DOJ monitors from polling sites but held DOJ to its concession that they're clerical staff without uniforms, guns or arrest powers. On Election Day an Ohio judge extended voting at a Toledo school after a bomb threat, which Secretary of State LaRose disputes (WTOL). A hearing on DHS election ads was reportedly set for Wednesday in New York (Inner City Press, unconfirmed).

Why it matters

Every court, now including the Supreme Court, has refused to let the federal government hold back ballots before they're counted. That narrows any fight over Alaska's close race to the normal count and post-election challenges.

How each side sees it

Voting-rights groups: the courts held the line

Democracy Docket and the NAACP Legal Defense Fund call the poll-monitor ruling a partial win and the Supreme Court denial the end of DOJ's pre-count strategy.

Via Democracy Docket (left, voting-rights) · CalMatters (nonpartisan)
Administration and allies: protecting eligible voters

DOJ argued the ballots of "likely noncitizens" should be segregated to protect eligible voters, and the en banc majority, written by Judge Eric Miller, let federal officials keep their stance toward election workers for now. Ohio's LaRose expects provisional ballots from the extension to be challenged.

Via SCOTUSblog (nonpartisan) · WTOL (local TV)
